ISAAC NEWTON

A farmer's son who preferred watermills and sundials to sheep, and who ended up rewriting the rules of the universe.

a body in motion stays in motion, unless acted on — his first law, still swinging

Lincolnshire, a farm called Woolsthorpe

A boy nobody expected much from

Newton was born prematurely on Christmas Day, 1642, three months after his father had died. He was so small, his mother later said, that he could have fit inside a quart pot.

When she remarried and moved away, he was left with his grandmother — and that early separation seems to have made him private for the rest of his life.

He was, by most accounts, an unremarkable student and a worse farmer. Sent home from school to manage the family land, he was found instead reading under a hedge or building small machines — a model windmill, a water clock, a sundial scratched into the wall of the house he grew up in. An uncle and a sympathetic schoolmaster noticed the boy was wasting his time on sheep, and pushed for him to go to university instead.

He arrived at Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1661, not as a prodigy but as a subsizar — a student who paid his way by working for wealthier ones. Nothing about his start suggested what was coming.

Cambridge closes its doors, 1665

Two years that changed everything

In 1665 the Great Plague reached Cambridge and the university sent its students home. For Newton, that meant going back to the same Woolsthorpe farm he'd once been so bad at running — except this time, alone with his books and no lectures to attend, something extraordinary happened.

In under two years he laid the groundwork for calculus, worked out how white light splits into colour, and began sketching the mathematics of what would become his theory of gravitation. He later called it his most productive period, and he was in his early twenties.

The story of the apple comes from these years — Newton himself told it, late in life, to friends who wrote it down. Whether or not fruit was literally involved, the idea he described was this: the same pull that brings an apple to the ground might reach all the way to the Moon.

The book that gave motion its rules

It took a visit from the astronomer Edmond Halley — who found Newton had already solved a problem the best minds in London were stuck on — to convince him to write it all down. The result, published in 1687, is still considered one of the most important scientific works ever written. In it, Newton set out three laws that describe how anything, anywhere, moves.

First Law

Inertia

An object at rest stays at rest, and one in motion keeps moving in a straight line, unless a force acts on it.

Second Law

Force and Mass

Force equals mass times acceleration — F = ma. Push harder, or push something lighter, and it speeds up faster.

Third Law

Action and Reaction

For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. Push on the world, and it pushes back.

The same book set out the law of universal gravitation — one equation describing why apples fall and why planets orbit the Sun, using the same force. It was the first time anyone had shown that the heavens and the Earth obey the same physics.

A prism, and the colour hidden in white light

Newton showed that ordinary white light is really a mixture of colours, split apart here in this page's own ink-and-brass palette rather than a true spectrum.

He worked this out during the same plague years, using nothing more than a glass prism, a darkened room, and a small hole cut into a shutter to let in a single beam of sunlight.

Royal Society, the Mint, Westminster Abbey

What he left behind

Newton spent his later years running things rather than discovering them — and did that with the same intensity.

As Master of the Royal Mint, he chased down counterfeiters personally, sometimes in disguise, at a time when coin forgery was punishable by death. As President of the Royal Society, he shaped British science for over twenty years.

He wasn't an easy man. He feuded bitterly with Robert Hooke over optics and with Gottfried Leibniz over who had invented calculus first — a dispute that split European mathematics for a generation. He was, by his own admission, more comfortable with ideas than with people.

But the ideas held up. His physics went unchallenged for over two hundred years, until Einstein — who still built on Newton's foundations rather than replacing them entirely.

Why I admire him

What gets me about Newton isn't really the equations — it's the two years at Woolsthorpe. Cambridge shut its doors, he had every excuse to just wait it out, and instead he taught himself calculus because the maths he needed didn't exist yet. That's the kind of curiosity I want to have: not curiosity that waits for a classroom, but curiosity that builds its own tools when it has to.

I also admire how patient his proof was. He didn't publish the Principia the moment he had a hunch about gravity — he spent years checking it against real measurements of the Moon and the planets before he trusted it enough to write it down. In a world that rewards being first and loud, there's something I respect about being right instead.
